## 1. Product overview
EquiMind is a private, non-diagnostic stress check-in and habit-building platform for Indian university students (UG and PG). A student answers **24 short statements** (drawn from a bank of **180**) about the last two weeks. It scores six life areas, writes a warm personal report, and turns the three areas under most pressure into a **14-day plan of five small daily activities**. A daily check-in, mood tracking, progress pages at Day 7 and Day 14, calming exercises, games, sounds and short reads keep the student engaged, while a school / campus counsellor can see who may need support and book sessions.

**Design principles**
- **Reflect, don't diagnose** — tentative, student-friendly language; no medical or psychological labels; no risk scores.
- **Small steps beat big plans** — every recommendation is a short, concrete activity with a reason it helps.
- **Private by default** — the report is the student's; counsellors see records only to offer support and act in severe cases.
- **Safety floor everywhere** — Tele-MANAS 14416 / 1800-891-4416 (24/7), 112 and campus health are always visible.
- **Warm and calm** — soft colours, illustrations, growth-stage avatars and encouraging copy.

## 3. Theoretical framework
EquiMind models student stress across **six life areas**, each split into **four sub-themes** (24 in total). The report explains *why* each pressure feels the way it does, then offers activities that target the easiest link to change.

### 3.1 Six life areas and 24 sub-themes
| Area | Sub-themes |
|---|---|
| **A · Academic & Workload** | A1 Workload volume & deadline pressure; A2 Overwhelm, avoidance & procrastination; A3 Concentration & cognitive strain; A4 Performance pressure & self-evaluation |
| **B · Body & Sleep** | B1 Sleep timing & quantity; B2 Sleep quality & disturbed sleep; B3 Daytime energy & fatigue; B4 Basic physical self-care |
| **C · Emotional Load & Coping** | C1 Overwhelm & distress intensity; C2 Anxiety & restlessness; C3 Low mood & motivation; C4 Avoidant / escape coping |
| **D · Social & Belonging** | D1 Loneliness & lack of honest connection; D2 Hiding distress & shame about help-seeking; D3 Relationship stress, breakup & conflict; D4 Belonging, homesickness & social fit |
| **E · Identity & Self-worth** | E1 Comparison & imposter feelings; E2 Self-criticism & 'not good enough'; E3 Rest-guilt & pressure to prove; E4 Self-doubt & uncertainty about ability |
| **F · Family, Money & Future** | F1 Money worry & financial pressure; F2 Family expectations & communication; F3 Future direction & career uncertainty; F4 Family money-talk & future-talk avoidance |

### 3.2 The theory behind each area (shown on the student's priority pages)

**Academic & Workload** — When too many deadlines sit in your head at once, your brain can't tell where to start — so it starts avoiding. The way out isn't working harder; it's making the first step so small you can't refuse it. Three visible things always beats a vague pile of 'everything'.

**Body & Sleep** — Sleep is where your brain repairs mood, memory and focus. When sleep is short or broken, everything else (attention, motivation, patience, feelings) runs on low battery. This isn't about discipline — it's about restoring the fuel. Your wake-up time, more than your bedtime, is what resets your body clock.

**Emotional Load & Coping** — When stress builds without a release, your body stays switched on — so small things feel big and rest doesn't feel restful. You can't think your way out of a racing body; calming the body first lets the thoughts follow. Naming what you feel, even with one word, can lower its intensity.

**Social & Belonging** — Carrying stress alone — especially after a breakup, a loss or a conflict with someone close — makes everything heavier. Grief after a relationship ends is real and takes real time; it's not a weakness. You don't need a therapist for your first honest conversation; you need one person and one honest sentence.

**Identity & Self-worth** — Comparison and self-criticism can feel like motivation, but they mostly drain the energy you need for the actual work. Your mind notices what is undone far more than what is done, so the picture you carry of yourself is skewed. Writing down what you did — every night — slowly corrects that picture.

**Family, Money & Future** — Money, family expectations and the future feel heavy because they seem too big to discuss and too important to ignore. Avoiding the conversation keeps the worry looping without any new information. One short, planned conversation usually shrinks the problem to its real size.

### 3.3 The stress loop
Stress is presented as a self-reinforcing cycle of four links (e.g. Pile-up & avoidance → Short, broken sleep → On edge & overwhelmed → Less energy to cope). The report names the student's loop from their own high-frequency answers and points to the link that is easiest to change first.

### 3.4 Why the activities work
Each activity is grounded in well-established behavioural principles — lowering the activation energy to start, protecting attention, anchoring the body clock, calming the body before the mind, naming emotions, sharing the load and collecting evidence of progress. The specific rationale for each activity appears in the Activity library (section 10).

### 3.5 Habit formation & growth stages
The plan runs for **14 days** with exactly **five activities a day**, ticked in a one-minute daily check-in. Progress is shown as a growing avatar with 14 illustrated stages (it never goes backwards) grouped into four phases:
| Phase | Days |
|---|---|
| Finding Calm | 1–3 |
| Feeling Happier & More Energetic | 4–8 |
| Connecting, Engaging & Enjoying | 9–12 |
| Confident, Social & Lively | 13–14 |

### 3.6 Worth ruling out: the body
Some ‘stress’ is partly physical. When sleep, energy or emotional scores are low, the report suggests simple checks:
| Possible cause | Signs | Simple check |
|---|---|---|
| Vitamin D | Low energy, low mood, aches, poor sleep — very common in students who stay indoors. | Vitamin D (25-OH) blood test |
| Vitamin B12 | Tiredness, brain fog, low mood. Common on vegetarian / vegan diets. | Vitamin B12 blood test |
| Iron (anaemia) | Tiredness, breathlessness, poor focus, hair fall. Common in young women; worth checking if periods are heavy. | CBC and ferritin / iron studies |
| Thyroid (TSH) | Tiredness, weight change, feeling cold — or anxiety and poor sleep. | One blood test rules it in or out |
| Meals & hydration | Afternoon crashes, mood swings, headaches from skipping meals, low protein or not enough water. | Three regular meals and water through the day |
| Movement & daylight | Low mood and poor sleep from very little walking or sunlight. | 20-30 min of either most days can help |

## 4. The assessment
- **Format:** 24 statements, one from each sub-theme, picked at random from the 180-item bank and shuffled.
- **Recall window:** the last two weeks.
- **Answer scale:** 0 = Never, 1 = Rarely, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Often, 4 = Almost always (higher = more stress).
- **Retakes:** Day 28–35 after the last check-in → the *same* 24 statements (a true comparison); within 7 days → fresh statements that exclude the previous ones; otherwise a fresh random set.
- **While the report is written** the student sees 20 rotating calming statements and mini-exercises.

## 6. Scoring & decision rules
| Rule | Definition |
|---|---|
| Area score | Each area has 4 statements (max raw 16). **Score = round(100 − raw ÷ 16 × 100)** — higher means less stress. |
| Overall score | Average of the six area scores. |
| Levels | < 35 **Needs care first** · 35–49 **Watch** · 50–64 **Steady** · 65+ **Strength** |
| Priority areas | The three lowest-scoring areas (ties broken by more ‘Often/Almost always’ answers). Each gets its own report page. |
| Strengths / resources | Up to two of the highest remaining areas scoring 50+. |
| Support path | **Path 2 (counsellor strongly suggested)** if any area < 35 or overall < 45; otherwise **Path 1** (self-help first, support available). |
| Body page | Shown if Body & Sleep < 50, Emotional Load & Coping < 55, 3+ fatigue statements answered Often/Almost always, or Path 2. |
| High statements | Up to 8 statements answered Often/Almost always feed the narrative and stress loop. |

## 7. AI-written narrative
- **Model:** Anthropic Claude (claude-sonnet-5-5) via the Emergent universal LLM key.
- **Input:** first name, six area scores and levels, overall score, three priority areas and the student's own high-frequency statements — nothing else.
- **Output (JSON):** an *honest line* (≤ 28 words), a 70–110-word narrative, a four-link stress loop and a loop note.
- **Guardrails:** tentative phrasing only ('Your responses suggest…'); a blocklist rejects any diagnosis or condition words (e.g. diagnose, depression, disorder, ADHD, suicide); invalid or blocked output falls back to a safe template written from the same data.

**Ready-to-copy scripts:**
- *To a counsellor:* “My sleep's been bad and things are piling up. I've mostly kept this to myself. I'd like help getting my routine back.”
- *To a parent (money):* “I've been feeling guilty about the course costs. Can we spend 20 minutes going through what's due and the options?”
- *To a faculty (deadline):* “Two submissions fall in the same week. Could we agree which to prioritise, or could I have a short extension on one?”

## 9. The 14-day plan & daily check-in
- **Plan construction:** Fixed wake-up time → the first activity of each priority area → 60-second check-in → fillers (the 5-minute brain dump, phone across the room, morning top-3, 4-7-8 breathing) until there are five.
- **Daily check-in pop-up** (once a day, from 24 h after the report): greeting + glow meter + today's mood (5 faces) → one step per activity (Yes / Not this time) → stress rating & feeling → a short feedback step.
- **Moods:** 0 Struggling · 1 Rough · 2 Mixed · 3 Steady · 4 Strong.
- **Calm Streak:** 14 named day badges, current and best streak, warm messages.
- **Week stats:** activities done of 35, % done, full days (all five done), best and hardest habit, average mood.

## 10. Activity library (26 activities)

### A · Academic & Workload

**The 10-minute start** — *10 min, once a day* · Best for: For the task you've been avoiding most
1. Pick the task you've been avoiding most. Write the first step in one sentence (e.g. 'open the lab manual and write the aim').
2. Set a 10-minute timer. Do only that step. Phone in another room.
3. When the timer rings, you're allowed to stop. Most of the time, you'll keep going.

*Why it helps:* Avoidance grows when a task feels big and vague. A tiny, timed start lowers the 'activation energy' — once you begin, the brain usually wants to finish what it started.

**Morning top-3** — *3 min, each morning* · Best for: Any day with more than one deadline
1. Before touching your phone, write 3 tasks that MUST be done today.
2. Everything else waits. Three realistic things beats ten imagined ones.
3. Tick each one off physically — the small win keeps the next task possible.

*Why it helps:* Your working memory can only hold a few things at once. Three clear priorities stop the day from feeling like an endless pile and give you quick, visible wins.

**Two 25-minute focus blocks** — *50 min, once a day* · Best for: When studying keeps turning into scrolling
1. Put your phone in another room and close every tab you don't need.
2. Work for 25 minutes on one task, then take a 5-minute break away from screens.
3. Repeat once. Two honest blocks beat a whole distracted evening.

*Why it helps:* Attention works in bursts. Short, protected focus blocks with real breaks help you learn more in less time and leave you less drained.

**Shutdown ritual** — *5 min, at a fixed stop time* · Best for: When work keeps spilling into the night
1. Pick a fixed time to stop academic work for the day.
2. Write down where you stopped and the very next step for tomorrow.
3. Close the laptop and say 'done for today' — the plan is safe on paper.

*Why it helps:* Without a clear stop, study keeps running in the background of your mind. A shutdown ritual tells your brain the plan is safe, so you can truly rest.

**Deadline map on one page** — *15 min, every 1-2 weeks* · Best for: When deadlines feel like a blur
1. List every submission, test and lab for the next two weeks on one page.
2. Mark the clashes and the one task that unlocks the most others.
3. Decide what to start first — and who to ask if two deadlines collide.

*Why it helps:* Seeing every deadline on one page turns a blurry worry into a concrete plan. Clashes become problems you can solve early instead of surprises.

### B · Body & Sleep

**Phone across the room** — *30 sec tonight* · Best for: Any night you want sleep back
1. Charge your phone across the room (or outside it) 30 minutes before you want to sleep.
2. Use a cheap alarm clock, or set the phone's alarm with volume loud enough.
3. If you need something to do, read a printed page or listen to music lying down.

*Why it helps:* Screens in bed keep the brain alert and delay sleep. Putting the phone out of reach removes the easiest reason to stay awake.

**The 5-minute brain dump** — *5 min, before bed* · Best for: When your mind won't switch off
1. Open a notebook. Write every worry, task and thought in your head — ugly, incomplete, unfiltered.
2. Circle the top 3 for tomorrow.
3. Close the book. The worries now live on paper, not in your head.

*Why it helps:* Unfinished thoughts keep the mind 'on'. Writing them down before bed parks them safely, which makes it easier to switch off and fall asleep.

**Fixed wake-up time** — *0 min, every morning* · Best for: When your sleep timing keeps drifting
1. Choose one wake-up time you can keep all 7 days (within 30 minutes).
2. Keep it on weekends too — this, more than bedtime, resets your body clock.
3. If you slept badly, still get up on time; nap no more than 20 minutes.

*Why it helps:* Your wake-up time sets your body clock. Keeping it steady — even after a late night — makes falling asleep easier and lifts daytime energy and mood.

**Daylight within 30 minutes of waking** — *10 min, each morning* · Best for: When mornings feel foggy
1. Within 30 minutes of waking, step onto a balcony or walk outside.
2. Stay 5-10 minutes without sunglasses if it's safe and comfortable.
3. Pair it with something you already do — tea, brushing, the walk to class.

*Why it helps:* Morning light is the strongest signal to your body clock. It sharpens alertness in the day and helps you feel sleepy at the right time at night.

**No caffeine after 2 pm** — *0 min, all day* · Best for: When you fall asleep late or wake often
1. Have your last tea, coffee or energy drink before 2 pm.
2. Swap the afternoon cup for water, a short walk or a snack.
3. Notice your sleep over a week rather than judging one night.

*Why it helps:* Caffeine stays in the body for many hours. Stopping by early afternoon protects deep sleep, even if you feel you can fall asleep anyway.

**The 20-minute get-up rule** — *As needed, at night* · Best for: When you lie awake and the thoughts get loud
1. If you've been awake in bed for about 20 minutes, get up.
2. Sit somewhere dim and do something calm and boring — no phone.
3. Go back to bed only when you feel sleepy again.

*Why it helps:* Lying awake and frustrated teaches the brain that bed is a place to worry. Getting up briefly and returning when sleepy rebuilds the bed–sleep link.

### C · Emotional Load & Coping

**4-7-8 breathing** — *1 min, when a loop starts* · Best for: When worry or tension starts to spiral
1. Breathe in through your nose for 4 counts.
2. Hold for 7 counts, then breathe out slowly through your mouth for 8 counts.
3. Do two rounds. Then name one thing you can see, hear and feel.

*Why it helps:* A long, slow out-breath activates the body's calming system. Calming the body first gives racing thoughts room to settle.

**60-second check-in** — *1 min, once a day* · Best for: Every day, especially busy ones
1. Rate your stress from 0 to 10.
2. Write one word for what you're feeling right now.
3. Notice it without fixing it. Patterns become clear within a week.

*Why it helps:* Naming how stressed you are and one feeling turns a vague heaviness into something you can understand. Labelling emotions is known to lower their intensity.

**Worry check on paper** — *5 min, when needed* · Best for: When one thought keeps replaying
1. Write the worrying thought exactly as it sounds in your head.
2. List the evidence for it and the evidence against it.
3. Write a fairer version of the thought — one you'd say to a friend.

*Why it helps:* Worries loop when they stay in your head. Writing one down and sorting it into 'can act / can't act' shrinks it to its real size.

**15 minutes of non-screen enjoyment** — *15 min, once a day* · Best for: When things feel flat or heavy
1. Pick something small you used to enjoy that doesn't need a screen.
2. Do it for 15 minutes — music, a walk, sketching, cooking, a game.
3. Notice how you feel after, even if you didn't feel like starting.

*Why it helps:* Stress narrows life down to tasks. Small, regular moments of enjoyment refill your energy and remind your mind that rest is allowed.

### D · Social & Belonging

**One honest sentence** — *2 min, once a day* · Best for: Any day you'd normally say 'I'm fine'
1. Pick one person you trust (friend, cousin, roommate). Not for advice — just to be heard.
2. Send one honest sentence: 'The week is a lot, and I've been keeping it to myself.'
3. That's it. You don't owe them a solution. You've just shared the weight.

*Why it helps:* Carrying stress alone makes it heavier. One honest sentence to a trusted person opens the door to support without needing a big conversation.

**Low-contact reset (if a breakup is weighing on you)** — *10 min, once this week* · Best for: When seeing their updates keeps re-opening the wound
1. Mute (not block) their social-media accounts and move the chat thread out of your main view.
2. Pick one person who will hear you vent without fixing — tell them you may need them this week.
3. Allow yourself a 'feel-it' hour: play one song, write one page, then do a 25-min focus block.

*Why it helps:* Seeing constant updates keeps reopening the hurt. Reducing contact for a while gives your mind space to heal at its own pace.

**Study next to someone** — *1 hour, twice a week* · Best for: When evenings feel lonely
1. Pick the library, a common room or a friend's desk instead of studying alone.
2. You don't need to talk — just work in the same space.
3. Take one short break together and ask how their week is going.

*Why it helps:* Simply being near others while you work eases loneliness and helps focus — connection without pressure to talk.

**A 15-minute call home** — *15 min, twice a week* · Best for: When you miss home or feel far away
1. Call someone at home at a time you're both free.
2. Share one real update — not just 'all fine'.
3. Ask them one question about their week too.

*Why it helps:* A short, regular call home keeps you connected to people who know you well, which can steady you when campus life feels far from home.

**Join one low-pressure activity** — *1 hour, once a week* · Best for: When you feel you don't quite fit in
1. Pick one recurring club, sport or group that has nothing to do with grades.
2. Go twice before deciding if it's for you.
3. Say hello to one person each time.

*Why it helps:* Belonging grows through small, repeated contact. One low-pressure activity creates familiar faces — the first step towards friendship.

### E · Identity & Self-worth

**Three-things-I-did list** — *3 min, each night* · Best for: When you feel you're never doing enough
1. Before bed, write three things you actually did today — however small.
2. Not what's undone. Only what you did.
3. Read last week's list on Sunday; notice the evidence.

*Why it helps:* The mind notices what's undone far more than what's done. Writing three things you did each night slowly corrects that picture and rebuilds self-belief.

**Worry check on paper** — *5 min, when needed* · Best for: When comparison or self-doubt keeps looping
1. Write the self-critical thought exactly as it sounds in your head.
2. List the evidence for it and the evidence against it.
3. Write a fairer version of the thought — one you'd say to a friend.

*Why it helps:* Worries loop when they stay in your head. Writing one down and sorting it into 'can act / can't act' shrinks it to its real size.

**15 minutes of non-screen enjoyment** — *15 min, once a day* · Best for: When rest feels like guilt
1. Pick something small you enjoy that doesn't need a screen.
2. Do it for 15 minutes — rest doesn't have to be earned.
3. Notice the guilt if it shows up, and do it anyway.

*Why it helps:* Stress narrows life down to tasks. Small, regular moments of enjoyment refill your energy and remind your mind that rest is allowed.

### F · Family, Money & Future

**20-minute money conversation** — *20 min, once this fortnight* · Best for: When money worry keeps looping
1. Ask a parent or guardian for 20 minutes at a calm time.
2. Use the script: 'Can we go through what's due and the options?'
3. Write down what you agreed and one next step.

*Why it helps:* Money worry loops when it stays unspoken. A short, planned conversation brings real information and usually makes the problem smaller.

**Deadline or plan conversation with faculty** — *10 min, once* · Best for: When deadlines or direction feel stuck
1. Email or visit a faculty member or mentor during office hours.
2. Share the clash or question in two sentences.
3. Ask which to prioritise, or whether a short extension is possible.

*Why it helps:* Faculty often have more flexibility than students expect. Asking early turns a stuck situation into a plan you can work with.

**One 15-minute talk with a senior** — *15 min, once a semester* · Best for: When the future feels unclear
1. Find a senior in your branch or programme you can approach.
2. Ask what they wish they'd known about placements, internships or next steps.
3. Write down one idea you can try this month.

*Why it helps:* Seniors have walked the path a year or two ahead of you. A short talk can turn a foggy future into a few clear next steps.
